The Appeal That Worked: What Winning Zoning Appeals Have in Common

Three decided appeals — a Missouri setback, a West Virginia fence, a Philadelphia warehouse — were all won the same way: on the record, the standard, and the findings. Not on rhetoric.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

Three members of the Weatherby Lake Board of Zoning Adjustment voted to grant the variance. Two voted against it. The variance needed four votes, so it failed — and then the Missouri Court of Appeals reversed the denial anyway. The board's attorney had spent the hearing reading portions of the town's ordinances aloud and commenting on what he believed they meant. Neither side had introduced the ordinances into evidence. No engineering report, no building plans, no documentation of what could or could not be built on the lot had been admitted either. The court did not rule on whether the variance should have been granted. It ruled that a board cannot deny a variance under an ordinance that was never in the record. The case, Weatherby Lake Improvement Company v. City of Weatherby Lake Board of Zoning Adjustment, decided August 20, 2024, is a small case about a setback. It is also a clean picture of how zoning appeals are actually won: not with rhetoric, but with the record.

The Weatherby Lake opinion, case number WD86685, is worth reading closely because both sides made mistakes and only one side's mistakes mattered. The applicant, Weatherby Lake Improvement Company, asked for a non-use variance from a front setback requirement and brought almost nothing to support it — no engineering report, no plans showing what could be built with the variance or without it. The board's attorney supplied the ordinances by summary, reading parts aloud, and the board adopted a written decision with optional findings to checkmark, supplied by that same attorney before the hearing. The court held that because the applicable municipal ordinances were not introduced into evidence, the board's decision and the circuit court's judgment affirming it had to be reversed, without prejudice to the applicant filing again. The applicant won by losing correctly: it attacked the procedure, not the merits, and the procedure could not survive review. The vote count — three in favor, two against, four required — shows how close the case was on the merits. The record shows why the merits never decided it.

A decade earlier, the Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia reversed a lower court in the opposite direction, and the pattern was the same. In Board of Zoning Appeals of the Town of Shepherdstown v. Borys M. Tkacz, No. 13-0688, decided September 30, 2014, the board had granted a variance to Patricia Kelch for a fence she had built, ordering her to lower it to six feet. Her neighbor, Borys Tkacz, appealed, and the circuit court reversed the board — finding that the board lacked jurisdiction, applied the wrong standard of proof, and was plainly wrong in its factual findings, and awarding Tkacz attorney's fees for what it called oppressive misconduct. Justice Loughry's opinion for the Supreme Court reversed the circuit court. The board, the court held, had acted within its authority; the circuit court had committed the error, not the board. Two lessons sit inside that reversal. First, the standard of review protects boards that follow it and exposes courts that do not. Second, the winning argument in both cases was never about the fence or the setback. It was about which body applied the right legal standard to the right record.

The third case completes the pattern, because this time the court reversed a grant. In Manayunk Neighborhood Council v. Zoning Board of Adjustment, City of Philadelphia, No. 157 C.D. 2008, decided December 18, 2008, Rector Street Associates had won variances from the Philadelphia board to build a residential addition onto a historic industrial warehouse. The neighborhood council appealed, and the Commonwealth Court reversed the grant. The board, Senior Judge Kelley wrote, had failed to make necessary findings of fact on unique hardship, on public detriment, and on whether the variance was the minimum that would afford relief. The trial court had compounded the error by relying on facts that were not in the board's decision at all. A board that grants a variance without writing down why — without findings on hardship, detriment, and minimum relief — hands its opponent the appeal. The applicant in that case had, in the court's words, provided ample indicia of hardship. It lost anyway, because the board's decision did not say so in findings a court could review.

Put the three cases together and the winning pattern is unmistakable, and it has almost nothing to do with persuasion. Winners attack the law the board applied, the record the board built, or the findings the board wrote. Weatherby Lake was the record: the ordinance was not in evidence. Shepherdstown was the standard: the wrong standard of proof, applied by the wrong body. Rector Street was the findings: no findings on hardship, detriment, or minimum relief, and a trial court inventing facts the board never found. The Michigan Court of Appeals stated the test the same way in Hughes v. Almena Township in 2009: a court affirms a zoning decision unless it did not comply with the constitution and laws of the state, was based on improper procedure, was not supported by competent, material, and substantial evidence on the record, or did not represent a reasonable exercise of the discretion granted by law. Four doors, and three of them open on procedure, evidence, and findings. Only the fourth — reasonable discretion — is about judgment, and it is the door courts open least.

The counterargument is that this picture flatters appellants. Most appeals lose, and they lose for the same structural reason winners win: courts defer to boards. The substantial-evidence test asks only whether a reasonable mind could accept the evidence as adequate, not whether the court agrees with the outcome. The District of Columbia's Board of Zoning Adjustment approves around 96 percent of the cases in its database of more than 14,000 — which means the typical appeal is not a fight against a hostile board but a request the board is inclined to grant, and the typical loss is a case that was weak on its own record. An appellant who arrives with no engineering report, as in Weatherby Lake, can win on the board's error and still have to file again, because the reversal was without prejudice. Procedure wins the round. It does not always win the project. The record cuts both ways, and a thin record from the applicant is as fatal as a thin record from the board — it simply takes longer to prove it.

The implication for anyone preparing an appeal is blunt and practical. Build the record as if the hearing is the only hearing, because it is: the court will not take new evidence, and the facts that are not in the transcript do not exist. Put the ordinance in evidence, in full, rather than letting anyone summarize it aloud. Demand findings on every element the statute requires — hardship, detriment, minimum relief — and if the board's written decision skips one, that omission is the appeal. Know the standard before the hearing, not after it, because the standard tells you which mistakes matter: an error of law, an improper procedure, a finding with no evidence behind it. Rhetoric has its place, and a good closing argument can move a board. But the appeals that win in court are won in the file. The board that writes a complete record and a complete decision is hard to beat. The board that does not has already written the appellant's brief.
