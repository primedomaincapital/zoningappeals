What Actually Wins Before a Zoning Board

Variances are granted about nine times in ten. That figure hides the real question: which applications fall into the tenth, and why.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

The hearing room in Jersey City holds sixty-seven applications in a year, and sixty-four of them leave approved. Three are denied, and two of those three denials are appeals, not variance requests at all. The board's own annual report puts the approval rate at 96 percent. A first-time applicant reading that number could be forgiven for thinking the hearing is a formality. It is not a formality. It is a test with a very particular shape, and the applicants who fail it usually fail it the same way.

Start with what a variance is. Zoning divides a city into districts and writes rules for each one: how tall a building may be, how far it must sit from the lot line, what uses are allowed. A variance is permission to break one of those rules for a particular lot, granted when the rule, applied to that lot, creates an unnecessary hardship. The classic case is physical: a lot so narrow, so steep, or so oddly shaped that nothing conforming can be built on it. The board does not grant a variance because the owner wants a bigger building. It grants one because the lot itself makes the rule unreasonable, and because the relief will not change the character of the neighborhood.

The numbers say boards grant that relief far more often than the doctrine suggests they should. John J. Infranca and Ronnie M. Farr, writing in the Pepperdine Law Review in 2023, put it plainly: variances are granted at high rates, often around ninety percent of applications, and in some jurisdictions in high volumes. Boston grants thousands of variances each year. The District of Columbia's Board of Zoning Adjustment approved around 96 percent of applications in its case database of more than 14,000 cases; in 2022 the figure hit 99 percent, with just two denials out of 194 decided cases, and since 2020 only 39 applications in total have been denied. Jersey City's 2021 report shows the same pattern: 64 approvals out of 67 applications heard, a 96 percent rate, with 166 variances granted across the cases heard and 49 of 50 use and density variances approved.

Not every city is so generous. ZoneWire's analysis of Atlanta's public meeting transcripts found 62 percent of 242 land-use decisions approved over two years, with variances clearing only 52 percent of 33 decisions. In Tulsa, 85 percent of 440 land-use decisions were approved, with variances at 66 percent of 64 decisions. The spread matters: a variance in Washington or Jersey City is close to a sure thing, while a variance in Atlanta is close to a coin flip. The difference is not the law. The statute in each place asks the same questions about hardship and neighborhood character. The difference is the board, the staff recommendation, and the record the applicant brings.

That is where the work is won. The applications that clear share a structure: a surveyor's plan showing the physical condition that makes the lot different from its neighbors, an engineer or architect who can explain why no conforming design works, and a showing that the relief asked for is the smallest that solves the problem. The applications that fail tend to ask for the largest relief first, offer no evidence beyond the owner's preference, and arrive without the abutter notices or the staff report answered. Boards deny few applications, but they deny the ones that give them nothing to write findings on — and a board that cannot write findings cannot grant a variance that survives court.

Special exceptions sit beside variances and run on a different logic. A special exception — called a conditional use in many states — is a use the ordinance already contemplates for the district, provided the applicant meets stated conditions: a church in a residential district, a daycare on a corner lot, a home business with limited traffic. The applicant does not have to prove hardship. The applicant has to prove the conditions are met, and the board's discretion is narrower. In Atlanta, special exceptions cleared 56 percent of the time against variances at 52 percent; in Tulsa, special exceptions cleared 87 percent. The distinction decides the strategy: an owner who can frame the request as a conditional use avoids the hardship test entirely, and the lawyer's first job is often to reframe the ask before the application is filed.

The counterargument is the one Infranca and Farr make themselves. If variances are granted nine times in ten, the variance is not an exception to the zoning code — it is the code, administered case by case, by a board with no planning staff and no accountability for the cumulative result. Boston's thousands of annual variances are, on this view, evidence that the underlying code is broken: setbacks and height limits written for a city that no longer exists, waived one lot at a time. The reformers' answer is to fix the code rather than the variance. The practitioner's answer is that the code will not be fixed this year, and the client needs the variance this year. Both are right, and the tension is the practice.

The implication for anyone who owns the hearing room is straightforward. A variance practice is not a lottery-ticket practice. It is a volume practice built on preparation: the same hardship showing, refined lot by lot, city by city, with the approval rates of each board known in advance and the staff's concerns answered before the hearing opens. The firms that do it well win the cases that are winnable and decline the ones that are not — and in a market where nine in ten applications are granted, the difference between a good firm and a careless one is the tenth case, the one that was always going to be denied. That case is the firm's reputation, and the client who loses it is the client who will tell the next owner to call someone else.
