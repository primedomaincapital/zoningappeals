How Long a Zoning Appeal Takes, from Filing to Decision

Connecticut gives the board 65 days to hear an appeal. Philadelphia takes about 180 days to schedule one. The delay that matters most is the continuance — and it is the one the applicant controls.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The construction loan commitment had ninety days left on it. The developer in the room knew the number by heart, because the lender's letter said the rate lock expired on a Tuesday in June and the extension, if granted, would cost a quarter point. The zoning board of appeals had not yet scheduled the hearing. The denial that started the appeal was four months old. The project was not losing its fight in the hearing room. It was losing it on the calendar, one month at a time, while the financing that made the project possible quietly ran out.

The statutes set a clock, and in some states the clock is short enough to read as a promise. Connecticut's General Statutes Section 8-7d requires a hearing to commence within sixty-five days after the board receives an appeal, the hearing to be completed within thirty-five days after it commences, and a decision to be rendered within sixty-five days after the hearing completes. The applicant may consent to extensions, but the total of all extensions cannot exceed sixty-five days. Massachusetts gives the board sixty-five days from receiving the notice to hold the hearing and one hundred days from filing to decide a variance, under Chapter 40A, Section 15. In Pinckney, Michigan, the zoning board of appeals must return a decision within sixty days of application. In Banner Elk, North Carolina, the board must schedule a hearing within thirty-six days of receiving the notice of appeal and decide within thirty days of the hearing. On paper, an appeal is a three- to four-month process. Connecticut's rule also defines receipt in a way that moves the whole schedule: the date of receipt is the day of the next regularly scheduled meeting after submission, or thirty-five days after submission, whichever is sooner — so a filing that lands the day after a meeting starts its clock weeks later than one that lands the day before. On paper, the financing clock and the appeal clock run at the same speed.

In practice, the hearing date is the first place the two clocks separate. Philadelphia's Zoning Board of Adjustment, according to the board's own manual as reported in March 2026, waits about 180 days for a regular hearing — six months from filing to the day anyone speaks. Paying the $995 accelerated hearing fee cuts that to about 140 days, saving roughly six weeks for a thousand dollars. The board usually issues its written notice of decision within 21 days of the vote. But a missed exhibit, a missed hearing, or a bad posting is among the most common reasons a hearing gets continued, and a continuance in Philadelphia usually costs four to six months. The District of Columbia's Board of Zoning Adjustment, whose database holds more than 14,000 cases, takes a median of about 99 days from filing to decision — down from a 204-day peak in 2016, and still more than three months. Roanoke, Virginia, tells applicants the process takes a minimum of one month, with applications due by 4 p.m. on the second Thursday of the month to be heard at the following month's hearing. Polk County, Florida, puts the whole appeal at two to three months, with the board typically reconvening one to two weeks after the hearing to reach its conclusion. The spread between the fastest and the slowest board is the difference between a project that survives and one that does not.

Then there is the step after the board, which is where timelines stop being measured in months and start being measured in years. In most states, a party has thirty days after the board's written decision to appeal to court — thirty days in Massachusetts after the decision is filed with the town clerk to reach Superior Court, thirty days in Polk County to reach Superior Court, thirty days in Philadelphia to reach the Court of Common Pleas, and fifteen days in Connecticut to reach Superior Court under General Statutes Section 8-8. The court step is not a second hearing. The court reviews the record the board made, and that review takes as long as litigation takes. Knox County, Tennessee, published the comparison in its own planning record: an appeal through the Board of Zoning Appeals typically takes one or two months, while Chancery Court takes twelve months or more. Of sixteen use-on-review appeals heard by that board since 2008, five continued to court and eleven did not — and 69 percent of the board's decisions were acceptable to both parties. The board is fast because it is the last forum where the facts are still open. The court is slow because by then the facts are closed and only the law is argued.

The delay that matters most to a project on a financing clock is not the one in the statute. It is the continuance. A hearing continued for a missing exhibit does not resume next week; in Philadelphia it resumes in four to six months, which is longer than the entire statutory schedule in Connecticut. A board that meets once a month turns a single missed deadline into a thirty-day slip, and a board that meets once a month with a full docket turns it into sixty. The applicant controls exactly one variable in this: whether the file is complete when it is filed. The survey, the photographs, the expert report, the notice to neighbors, the posting on the property — every item the board's manual lists is a continuance that did not happen. Developers who treat the filing checklist as paperwork lose months to it. Developers who treat it as the schedule keep the schedule.

There is an argument for slowness, and it deserves more than a nod. A board that decides in thirty days has heard less than a board that takes ninety. Neighbors need time to organize, to hire their own expert, to read the traffic study rather than hear it summarized. A rushed record is a thin record, and a thin record is the kind a court reverses — the Missouri case in this site's companion article on winning appeals was reversed precisely because the board decided on ordinances that were never put in evidence. Speed bought at the price of the record is not speed. It is a loss deferred to the court step, where it costs more and takes longer. The boards that take six months are not all failing. Some of them are doing the work that keeps their decisions standing.

So the question for anyone with a project on a financing clock is not how long an appeal takes in the abstract. It is how long this appeal takes, in this jurisdiction, on this board's calendar, with this file. The answer has four parts, and they should all be known before the appeal is filed: the statutory deadlines, the board's actual hearing wait, the cost of a continuance, and the date the court appeal must be filed if the board says no. The accelerated hearing fee in Philadelphia buys about forty days for $995 — cheap, if forty days is what the loan needs, and worthless if the file is incomplete and the hearing gets continued anyway. The appeal that survives is usually the one filed on day two with a complete record, not the one filed on day twenty-nine with a promise to supplement. In zoning appeals, the calendar is the case. The financing clock does not pause for it.
