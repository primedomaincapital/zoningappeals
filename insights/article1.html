Thirty Days to Save a Project

In most states the clock on a zoning appeal runs thirty days. In Connecticut, the trip to court runs fifteen. Cases are won and lost before the first hearing.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

The letter arrives on a Tuesday. A developer in Manchester, New Hampshire, has spent fourteen months assembling a parcel, paying for engineering, and sitting through planning-board workshops for a forty-unit building. The envelope from the zoning administrator is thin. Inside, a single page: the permit is denied. The project is dead — unless the developer's lawyer files an appeal to the zoning board of appeals within thirty days. Day one is the day the decision is made. Day thirty-one is the day the denial becomes permanent, no matter how wrong it was.

That is the zoning appeal in its plainest form: a short, unforgiving window during which a bad decision can still be undone. New Hampshire gives thirty days to reach the board of appeals under RSA 676:5. Pennsylvania's Municipalities Planning Code gives thirty days to appeal a zoning officer's decision. Massachusetts gives thirty days to appeal to the permit-granting authority under Chapter 40A, Section 15, and then twenty days after the board's decision is filed with the town clerk to take the matter to Superior Court. Connecticut is the outlier that proves the rule: an aggrieved party has fifteen days from publication of the decision to commence an appeal to Superior Court under General Statutes Section 8-8. The numbers differ by state. The logic does not. The appeal period is short by design, and the courts enforce it without sympathy for the party who filed late.

What makes the deadline so consequential is what happens inside it. An appeal to the zoning board is not a formality. The board holds a hearing — in Massachusetts, within sixty-five days of receiving the notice — and must decide a variance within one hundred days of filing. The board can uphold, reverse, or modify the decision below, and its written decision becomes the record a court will later review. The District of Columbia's Board of Zoning Adjustment, whose case database holds more than 14,000 cases, takes a median of about 99 days from filing to decision — down from a peak of 204 days in 2016, and still more than three months of delay for a project whose approval is, in the District's own numbers, close to certain. A developer who files on day twenty-nine has already spent most of the window; a developer who files on day two has the time to build the record that wins.

The record matters because the standard of review is narrow. Courts do not re-hear a zoning appeal from scratch. They ask whether the board's decision was supported by substantial evidence, whether it was arbitrary, and whether the board followed its own procedures. The facts that decide that question are the ones placed before the board at the hearing: the surveyor's measurements, the engineer's testimony, the photographs of the lot's slope or shape, the minutes showing what the neighbors actually said. A lawyer who walks into the hearing with a thin file is asking the board to grant relief on faith. A lawyer who walks in with a complete record is giving the board the findings it needs to write a decision that survives court.

The stakes behind the clock keep rising. The Census Bureau counted 1,431,616 new privately owned housing units authorized by building permits in 2025, with a total valuation of $378.99 billion — down 3.1 percent from the 1,478,000 units authorized in 2024. Every one of those units passed through a local zoning process first, and the projects that did not make the count include the ones stopped at the counter. Freddie Mac put the national housing shortfall at 3.7 million homes as of the third quarter of 2024, and the Congressional Research Service's December 2025 review of shortage estimates put the range at roughly 4 million to 5 million homes. When a denied permit kills forty units, the loss is not abstract. It is forty homes that a short market does not get, and a developer who has already paid for the land.

There is a counterargument, and it deserves its weight. Short deadlines exist to protect finality. A neighbor who wants to build a fence, a town that wants to plan its roads, a buyer who wants to know whether the lot next door will stay residential — all of them need to know when a decision is final. A six-month appeal window would leave every permit in limbo and every project financed on a guess. The fifteen-day Connecticut window and the thirty-day windows elsewhere are the price of certainty, and the courts have repeatedly refused to bend them for sympathetic facts. The system is not broken because it is strict. It is strict because the alternative is worse.

That answer is right as far as it goes, and it is exactly why the work belongs to specialists. A deadline that cannot be extended is a deadline that must be calendared, and the firms that do this work keep it calendared the way surgeons keep a schedule: the day the decision issues, the day the notice is published, the day the hearing must be requested, the day the record closes. The client who finds that firm on the day the denial arrives is the client whose project survives. The client who finds it a week late is the client who learns, too late, that the merits never mattered. In zoning appeals, the calendar is the case.
