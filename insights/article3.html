The Land-Use Bar Is Consolidating

The biggest firms in the country now run land-use practices measured in dozens of lawyers and billions in revenue — while thousands of boutiques still own the hearing room.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

The conference room on the forty-second floor looks out over Biscayne Bay, and the lawyers in it are not arguing a case. They are counting. Holland & Knight's land-use group counts 66 attorneys. Greenberg Traurig's land-use practice counts offices in Miami, New York, Long Island, Boston, Philadelphia, and Wilmington, and the firm's Miami team alone has spent six decades shaping South Florida's growth. The work on the table is a $4 billion mixed-use project in Anaheim, a $2.2 billion stadium replacement for the Tennessee Titans in Nashville, and a $1 billion, six-acre project on the Southwest waterfront in Washington. The land-use bar used to be a collection of solo practitioners who knew the town clerk by name. It is now, at the top, a national practice measured in billions.

The money behind the consolidation is visible in the firms' own results. Greenberg Traurig reported gross revenue of $2.62 billion in 2024, up 13.7 percent year over year, with revenue per lawyer at $991,000 and profit per equity partner at $2.63 million. Holland & Knight reported $2.04 billion, up 10.5 percent. Those are firm-wide figures, and land use is one practice among many — but the land-use groups at both firms are the ones advising on the largest single projects in the country, and the Legal 500 ranks Holland & Knight's land-use practice as tier one nationally. The work highlights read like a development pipeline: OCVIBE, a $4 billion mixed-use project in Anaheim; the Titans' new $2.2 billion stadium; The Stacks, a $1 billion project delivering 2 million square feet on the District's Southwest waterfront.

What drives the consolidation is the same force that drives the market for the land itself. The Census Bureau counted 1,431,616 new privately owned housing units authorized by building permits in 2025 — down 3.1 percent from 1,478,000 in 2024, and far below the 2005 peak of 2,155,316 — while Freddie Mac put the national housing shortfall at 3.7 million homes as of the third quarter of 2024, Zillow at 4.7 million for 2023, and the National Association of Realtors at 5.5 million. The gap between what is permitted and what is needed is the space where land-use lawyers work: every unit in that shortfall has to pass a zoning board, a planning commission, or a city council first, and the projects big enough to close the gap are the ones that need a national firm's bench. A developer building forty units in one town hires the boutique down the street. A developer building four thousand units across six cities hires the firm with a land-use team in each one.

The boutique is not dying. It is the other half of the market, and it owns the hearing room in a way the national firms do not. A zoning board of appeals is a local body: five volunteers, a town planner, a clerk who has kept the minutes for twenty years. The lawyer who has appeared before that board forty times knows which member worries about traffic, which one worries about drainage, and which one will vote no unless the abutters are in the room. That knowledge does not scale, and it does not travel. The national firm's advantage is the opposite: the capacity to run a dozen entitlements at once, the relationships with the agencies that review environmental impact and historic preservation, the ability to staff a $4 billion project without blinking. The market splits along that line, and both sides are growing.

The third force in the room is the one neither side controls. Existing-home sales fell to a 3.75 million seasonally adjusted annual rate in October 2026, down 8.8 percent from a year earlier and the slowest pace since August 2010, with the median existing-home price at $416,700 and the average 30-year fixed mortgage above 7 percent. When sales stall and rates stay high, developers slow down, and the entitlement pipeline narrows. The firms that feel it first are the ones whose work is tied to the next project, not the one already approved. The counterweight is the shortfall itself: a country short roughly 4 million to 5 million homes does not stop needing entitlements because rates are high. It needs them more, and the states are responding — recently passed state housing laws were estimated in November 2026 to add roughly 103,000 homes a year nationwide, a figure that sounds large until it is set against a hole of 3.7 million.

There is a counterargument to the consolidation story, and the boutiques make it themselves. A national practice is expensive, and its clients are the developers who can afford it. The thousands of small matters — the denied permit on a single lot, the variance for a garage that sits six inches too close to the line, the special exception for a daycare — are the work of the local firm, and there are thousands of them for every OCVIBE. The boutiques also keep the relationships that win hearings, and a national firm that flies a partner in for one hearing cannot buy that. The consolidation at the top does not shrink the base. It leaves the base to the firms that live there, and the base is where the volume is.

The implication is that the land-use market is two markets wearing one name, and the domain that serves them has to speak to both. The national firms need a brand that reads as authoritative to a developer choosing counsel for a billion-dollar entitlement. The boutiques need a brand that reads as exact to the owner searching, under a thirty-day deadline, for the lawyer who has stood in front of their board before. A name that states the service in two words does both jobs at once, because the service is the same at both ends of the market: the appeal, filed on time, argued by someone who knows the room. The firms are consolidating. The search is not.
