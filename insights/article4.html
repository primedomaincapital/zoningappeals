What a Zoning Appeal Costs: Fees, Lawyers, and the Expert Witness

The filing fee in Philadelphia is $811. In Los Angeles it is $15,811. The fee is the smallest check in the appeal — the lawyer, the expert, and the continuance cost the rest.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The refusal letter from Philadelphia's Department of Licenses and Inspections arrived with a number printed at the bottom: $811. That was the fee to appeal to the Zoning Board of Adjustment, and it looked, on its face, like the whole price of fighting back. It was not. By the time the developer's appeal was decided, the $811 check was the smallest one written. The lawyer's invoices ran past $20,000. A traffic engineer billed for a study and a day of testimony. A continuance cost another $177 just to re-post the notice. The filing fee is the price of admission. The appeal is everything after the door.

Start with the published fee, because it is the only number that does not move. Philadelphia's Zoning Board of Adjustment, under a fee schedule that took effect August 1, 2026, charges $125 for a property with an existing one- or two-family dwelling and $811 for every other property. An accelerated hearing costs $995 per property, capped at $2,985 for combined hearings. Denver's Board of Adjustment charges between $100 and $400 depending on the category, with an appeal of an administrative decision set at $400. La Crosse, Wisconsin, charges a flat $400 filing fee for a variance or administrative appeal. Zionsville, Indiana, charges $320 for an administrative appeal, $870 plus $220 per additional request for a residential use variance, and $1,200 plus $300 per additional request for a non-residential use variance. These are the cheap jurisdictions. Los Angeles is not one of them: under Section 19.01 of the Los Angeles Municipal Code, a first-level appeal by the applicant costs 85 percent of the underlying application fees or $15,811, whichever is less, and an additional level of appeal costs $11,585. A variance application in Los Angeles costs $13,283 before anyone appeals anything. A neighbor who appeals as an aggrieved person rather than the applicant pays $158 in Los Angeles — a deliberate asymmetry that keeps the door open to the public while charging the developer the real cost of the hearing.

The lawyer is the second line, and it is usually the largest. LegalClarity, a firm that publishes cost guides for zoning work, puts land-use attorney rates at $150 to $500 or more per hour, with a straightforward variance running about $2,000 in total legal fees and a contested rezoning or appeal running $15,000 or more. The same firm's figures put a zoning appeal or litigation at $10,000 to $50,000 and beyond, with discovery, motions, expert testimony, and trial preparation pushing the total past whatever the underlying application cost. A court appeal of a board decision commonly starts at $5,000 in attorney fees and runs to $20,000 or more depending on complexity and whether the case reaches trial. Flat fees exist for the simple cases — $1,000 to $5,000 for a single hearing with minimal negotiation — but they disappear the moment a neighbor hires counsel, the board continues the hearing, or the case moves to court. The hourly meter does not stop because the board is slow. It runs faster, because every continuance is another round of preparation. A straightforward residential variance, the firm's second guide notes, typically costs $1,500 to $5,000 once application fees and basic professional services are counted — and the range is wide because every dollar in the process is set locally, by the city or county that controls the fee, dictates the reports, and decides how many hearings the case gets.

The expert witness is the third line, and the one most appellants under-budget. LegalClarity puts planners, traffic engineers, and environmental consultants at $150 to $400 per hour for testimony, billed separately for preparation time and appearance time. A traffic study for a mid-size project is not a letter; it is counts, modeling, and a report that the board's own engineer can cross-examine. A planner testifies on hardship, on the character of the neighborhood, on whether the request is the minimum relief that solves the problem. The expert is not decoration. Courts reviewing a board decision ask whether it rests on substantial evidence, and the evidence that survives that test is usually the expert's. An appellant who arrives with a lawyer and no expert is asking the board to grant relief on argument alone. Boards rarely do, and when they do, the decision is the kind a court reverses — the kind this site's companion article on winning appeals examines case by case.

Then come the charges nobody puts in the first budget. Philadelphia charges $177 to re-post a notice for each continuance and $151 when an applicant asks to amend a pending application. North Franklin Township in Pennsylvania charges a $500 residential variance appeal fee or $600 for all other properties, plus the stenographer's appearance fee for the hearing and for each continuation, plus a per-page transcript fee. Alachua County, Florida, charges $1,000 per day for a hearing officer to hear and decide appeals. Lake County, Montana, charges $550 to $600 for an appeal of an administrative decision, plus $15 per adjacent address for certified-mail notice of the hearing. None of these is large on its own. Together, on a case that runs to two hearings and a transcript, they add thousands to a bill that started at $811. And behind all of it sits the cost that never appears on an invoice: the carrying cost of a project that cannot start. Land payments, loan interest, and option deadlines keep running while the board's calendar turns.

There is a defense of the fee structure, and it is not frivolous. Fees screen out appeals filed on a whim. Los Angeles charging an applicant $15,811 while charging an aggrieved neighbor $158 is a deliberate choice: the party asking the city to re-decide its own ruling pays for the re-decision, while the public keeps a cheap route to challenge one. Flat, low fees in small towns keep the process open to homeowners who cannot hire a firm. The counterweight is that the screen works unevenly. A homeowner fighting a fence can afford $125 in Philadelphia. A small developer fighting a denial on a forty-unit building faces $811 plus a lawyer, an expert, and months of carrying cost — and the fee is the least of it. The published fee is democratic. The real spend is not, because the real spend is set by how hard the other side fights, and that is the one variable no fee schedule controls.

The practical way to price an appeal, then, is as three separate budgets, not one. The government budget is the filing fee plus the continuance and transcript charges — know it before filing, because it is printed and it does not negotiate. The professional budget is the lawyer and the expert — get it in writing, ask what a continuance costs, and ask what the court step costs before the board step begins, because the second decision is the one that doubles the first. The time budget is the carrying cost of the project while the appeal runs — the number that turns a $25,000 appeal into a $100,000 problem if the hearing slips by six months. Anyone who can price all three before filing has already done the most useful work in the appeal. Anyone who prices only the filing fee is the person who discovers, halfway through, that the $811 check was never the price. It was the deposit.
