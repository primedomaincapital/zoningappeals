Zoning appeal deadlines, variance approval rates, and housing permits, by the numbers

Zoning appeal deadlines by state, variance and special-exception approval rates, housing permit volumes, the national housing shortfall, and land-use firm revenues — from published sources.

Three different kinds of number sit behind this name, and they must not be mixed. The first kind measures the clock: the days a property owner has to appeal, which decide whether the merits are ever heard. The second kind measures the hearing: how often boards grant variances and special exceptions, which decides what the appeal is worth. The third kind measures the demand: the homes being permitted and the homes still missing, which decide how much work there is to do. The first kind tells a reader how urgent the client is. The second tells a reader how the case is likely to end. The third tells a reader how large the market is.

What the latest numbers show is a practice built on short deadlines and high grant rates: thirty days to appeal in most states, fifteen days to reach court in Connecticut, variances granted about nine times in ten — and a housing market short roughly 4 million to 5 million homes, with 1.43 million units permitted in 2025, each one a project that passed through zoning first.

How to read these numbers

Included: state appeal deadlines as written in statute and in official guidance (New Hampshire RSA 676:5, Pennsylvania MPC §1002-A, Massachusetts Chapter 40A, Connecticut General Statutes §8-8); variance and special-exception approval rates from the Pepperdine Law Review empirical study, the DC Office of Zoning case database, the Jersey City Zoning Board of Adjustment annual report, and ZoneWire's transcript analyses for Atlanta and Tulsa; Census Bureau Building Permits Survey annual figures for 2025; housing-shortfall estimates as compiled by the Congressional Research Service; National Association of Realtors existing-home sales for October 2026; and the published 2024 revenues of Greenberg Traurig and Holland & Knight. Excluded: any claim about what a single board will do in a single case, since approval rates vary sharply by city; any permit figure presented as a completed home, since a permit is an authorization, not a build; and any law-firm revenue figure presented as land-use revenue, since the published figures are firm-wide.

Deadlines are stated as written in the statute; the date from which the clock runs (decision date, filing date, or publication date) varies by state and by the type of decision, and local ordinances can set shorter periods. Approval rates are board-level aggregates, not predictions for an individual application: Atlanta's variance rate of 52 percent and the District of Columbia's 96 percent describe different boards, different codes, and different caseloads. Permit figures are authorizations reported by local offices to the Census Bureau; the 2025 annual total of 1,431,616 is the revised annual figure, 0.4 percent above the preliminary estimate. Housing-shortfall estimates differ because each researcher defines a healthy market differently; the range, not any single number, is the finding.
